## The Triple Dilemma (Trilemma)

**Ecological improvement ↔ Macro contraction**
Every scenario delivering meaningful ecological gains generates output contraction (or limited macro improvement). The Leontief structural factor (l_{r1,r2} − l_{r1,r1}) ≈ −1 that drives ecological improvement is the same factor driving gross output reduction. Structurally inseparable under current technology.

**Macro contraction ↔ Distributional regression**
When output falls, employment falls disproportionately in sectors with lower labour intensity than those they replace. Wage share falls, profit share rises — cost concentrated on workers. Strongest in intermediate production shifts, especially energy.

**Ecological improvement ↔ EU–RoW asymmetry**
EU ecological gains are partly purchased through reduced demand for RoW exports (especially energy). Stronger EU ecological improvement → stronger RoW demand contraction for historically exported goods. No scenario produces strong EU ecological improvement with neutral/positive RoW employment.

## Regime-Specific Findings

### Final Demand Shifts (Scenarios 1–6)

**Low leverage overall**, with one notable exception:

- **Scenario 1 (Food/Meat):** Employment effect (−0.11%) is 5× larger than any other final demand scenario. Driven by the large consumption share of Meat (β = 3.84%) and the high employment intensity differential between Meat (n/x = 5.37) and Other Food (n/x = 4.35). Also produces meaningful ecological savings (mat −0.63%, emis −0.52%). *This scenario stands apart from the rest of the final demand group.*

- **Scenario 2 (Household Energy):** Strongest ecological effect in the group (emis −1.0%). Positive current account (energy import substitution). Near-neutral employment. *Best-performing final demand scenario overall.*

- **Scenarios 3, 5, 6:** Essentially zero effect — target sectors have negligible shares in the allocation vectors.

- **Scenario 4 (Government Cement):** Small negative employment (−0.02%), slight ecological leakage to RoW (emis and land slightly positive in Z2).

### Intermediate Production Shifts (Scenarios 7–13)

**Strong ecological gains, contractionary dynamics:**

- **Scenario 7 (Wood):** Moderate mat reduction (−0.29% LT), small employment decline (−0.02%). Most contained of the production shocks.

- **Scenario 8 (Pulp):** Very small effects across all dimensions. Pulp sector has minimal A-matrix weight.

- **Scenario 9 (Plastics):** Small employment decline (−0.02% LT). Mat slightly increases (secondary plastics more material-intensive). Recycling falls.

- **Scenario 10 (Metal) — Anomalous:** Expansionary (go +0.04%, n +0.03% LT). Material savings are large (mat −0.94%), but land and emissions *increase* (+0.34%) — structural rebound in land/emissions dimension. Z2 loses employment (asymmetric). Secondary metal sector has higher land/emissions intensity than primary in this model.

- **Scenarios 11 (Glass), 12 (Cement):** Near-zero macro/social effects. Small ecological gains. Sectors too small in the A-matrix to generate significant economy-wide effects.

- **Scenario 13 (Energy) — Highest leverage:** Strongest ecological gains (emis −1.95% LT, rec −3.87% LT). Strong current account improvement (+2.26% LT). But distributionally regressive: wage share −0.10%, profit share +0.18% LT. Z2 employment falls (−0.02% LT). Most asymmetric EU–RoW outcome. Requires the most robust complementary policy response.

## Cross-Border Transmission

The bilateral MRIO structure makes explicit what single-region models miss:

1. **EU intermediate restructuring reduces Z1 demand for Z2 intermediate exports** (off-diagonal Leontief entries l_{Z2,Z1}), directly contracting RoW gross output.

2. **Energy scenario produces most asymmetric outcome:** EU's energy import substitution reduces RoW energy export revenues. EU ecological gain is partly borne by RoW trading partners.

3. **Ecological leakage is partial but present:** When EU demand for primary materials falls, RoW producers face demand contraction — their own emissions may fall (good) but also their income (bad). The model gives a first-order partial equilibrium estimate; relative price adjustment in RoW markets is abstracted away.

## Policy Implications

1. **Intermediate production restructuring requires fiscal stabilisation.** Strongest ecological gains coincide with output/employment contraction → CE industrial policy must be paired with automatic stabilisers or labour market support.

2. **Final demand reallocation alone is insufficient.** Consumer-side CE policies produce only marginal ecological improvement. Production-side leverage is categorically larger.

3. **Cross-border effects require coordinated policy.** EU unilateral CE restructuring transfers economic costs to RoW — particularly in energy. Border carbon adjustment or international coordination is necessary to prevent burden shifting.

4. **Energy restructuring is highest-leverage, highest-risk.** Scenario 13 delivers strongest ecological gains + sharpest social regression + most asymmetric cross-border burden. Requires most robust complementary policy.

5. **Food consumption shift (Scenario 1) is underappreciated.** Produces meaningful ecological savings (comparable to several production scenarios) but also significant employment cost — the food sector is employment-intensive. Should not be lumped with the near-zero final demand scenarios.
