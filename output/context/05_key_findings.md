## The Triple Dilemma (Trilemma)

**Ecological improvement ↔ Macro contraction**
Every scenario delivering meaningful ecological gains generates output contraction (or near-zero macro improvement). The Leontief structural factor (l_{r1,r2} − l_{r1,r1}) ≈ −1 that drives ecological improvement is the same factor driving gross output reduction. Structurally inseparable under current technology.

**Exception/complication — Construction (Sc5, Sc14):** Secondary construction is more emission-intensive than primary. These scenarios show material savings but ecological damage — the trilemma is inverted (improvement in one ecological dimension at cost of another, and expansionary macro).

**Exception — Metal (Sc10):** Expansionary AND material savings, but land/emissions increase (different structural rebound).

**Macro contraction ↔ Distributional regression**
When output falls, employment falls disproportionately in sectors with lower labour intensity than those they replace. Wage share falls, profit share rises — cost concentrated on workers. Strongest in Sc13 (Energy: shw −0.10%, shp +0.18%).

**Ecological improvement ↔ EU–RoW asymmetry**
EU ecological gains are partly purchased through reduced demand for RoW exports (especially energy, Sc13). Stronger EU improvement → stronger RoW demand contraction for historically exported goods.

## Construction Emission Rebound (Key Finding)

Both Sc5 (FD, ρ=0.05) and Sc14 (Int, ρ=0.05) show large emission *increases* despite material savings:

| Scenario | mat LT.Z1 | emis LT.Z1 | land LT.Z1 |
|----------|-----------|------------|------------|
| Sc5 (FD) | −0.84% | **+5.19%** | **+5.19%** |
| Sc14 (Int) | −0.35% | **+2.15%** | **+2.15%** |

**Mechanism:** Secondary construction sector (renovation, retrofitting) is more energy/emission-intensive per unit output than primary (new build) at current calibration. Shifting demand to secondary construction produces material savings (less concrete, steel) but much more energy use per unit. This is a structural feature of the model that would require targeted energy decarbonisation of the renovation sector to resolve.

## Four Cross-Border Transmission Patterns (MRIO Contribution)

The bilateral MRIO structure identifies four distinct cross-border channels:

### Pattern 1: Symmetric Contraction (most Intermediate scenarios)
EU intermediate restructuring reduces Z1 demand for Z2 intermediate exports (off-diagonal Leontief entries l_{Z2,Z1}). Both regions contract, broadly proportionally. Seen in Sc7, Sc8, Sc11, Sc12.

### Pattern 2: Asymmetric Sign Reversal (Sc10 Metal)
EU expands while RoW contracts. Secondary metal sector has lower domestic material requirements but sources more from RoW substitutes in different categories — EU gains, RoW loses employment. Asymmetric because secondary metal substitution benefits EU's terms of trade.

### Pattern 3: Fiscal Dividend in Energy Scenarios (Sc2, Sc13)
EU and Z2 government balances *both improve* (gdef positive). EU reduces energy imports → improved TB, CAB; reduced fossil fuel subsidy burden. Z2 fiscal improvement comes through different channels (reduced energy-related transfers). EU b_s (bonds) falls, Z2 b_s rises — redistribution of government liabilities.

### Pattern 4: CAB/TB Driven by Fossil Import Substitution (Sc2, Sc5, Sc13)
Scenarios that substitute domestic energy (Sc2: HH energy, Sc13: production energy) or construction (Sc5) show large positive current account improvements (+1.27% to +2.88% LT). Mechanism: reduced import demand for fossil fuels (or primary construction materials) from Z2 → bilateral trade balance improvement. This is a structural leakage reduction, not a demand expansion.

## Regime-Specific Findings

### Final Demand Shifts (Scenarios 1–6)

Not uniformly "low leverage" — within-group heterogeneity is large:

- **Sc1 (Food/Meat):** Employment effect (−0.11%) is 5× larger than any other FD scenario. Meaningful ecological savings. Employment cost concentrated on workers (shw −0.03%). Should not be grouped with near-zero scenarios.
- **Sc2 (Energy):** Best ecological-macro combination in FD group (emis −1.0%, CAB +1.27%, near-neutral employment).
- **Sc5 (Construction):** Macroeconomically positive but ecologically catastrophic (emis +5.19%). Single-region analysis blind to Z2 employment contraction (−0.03% LT).
- **Sc3, Sc6:** Near-zero — negligible sector shares in relevant allocation vectors.
- **Sc4 (Gov Plastics):** Small, mixed ecological signal.

### Intermediate Production Shifts (Scenarios 7–14)

Strong leverage, heterogeneous outcomes:

- **Sc13 (Energy):** Highest leverage, most asymmetric. Strongest ecological + fiscal gains, strongest distributional regression, strongest Z2 burden.
- **Sc10 (Metal):** Anomalous expansion, but ecological partial rebound (land/emis).
- **Sc14 (Construction):** Structural rebound — same as Sc5 but smaller magnitude (ρ=0.05, smaller A-matrix entry).
- **Sc7 (Wood), Sc9 (Plastics):** Moderate, well-behaved.
- **Sc8, Sc11, Sc12:** Near-negligible.

## Policy Implications

1. **Intermediate production restructuring requires fiscal stabilisation.** Sc7–Sc13 (excl. Construction): ecological gains coincide with contraction → CE industrial policy must pair with automatic stabilisers or labour market support.

2. **Channel specificity matters.** Production-side leverage (Sc13 > Sc7 > Sc9) is categorically higher than demand-side for ecological outcomes, but demand-side Sc1 and Sc2 have non-trivial co-benefits. Policy design should distinguish by channel, not just production vs. demand.

3. **Cross-border effects require coordinated policy.** EU unilateral CE restructuring transfers economic costs to RoW — particularly in energy (Sc13) and construction (Sc5). Border adjustment or international coordination needed.

4. **Construction emission rebound requires concurrent technology policy.** Scenarios 5 and 14 fail ecologically at current calibration. Secondary construction must be decarbonised (heat pumps, renewable energy in renovation) before material-substitution policies work as intended.

5. **Energy restructuring (Sc13) is highest-leverage, highest-risk.** Strongest ecological gains + sharpest social regression + most asymmetric cross-border burden. Requires most robust complementary policy.
